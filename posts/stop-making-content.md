There is a strange pressure that comes with being a photographer online. You always need something to post: a new photograph, a reel, a behind-the-scenes video, a carousel, a story, a location guide, a before-and-after edit, anything that keeps the account alive.
<br/><br/>
Because the internet moves so quickly, it can begin to feel as though you are not a photographer unless you are constantly producing. That changes the way you work. Instead of asking, "What would be interesting to photograph today?" you start asking, "What can I post today?"
<br/><br/>
They sound similar. They are not.
<br><br>

## Photography Isn't a Content Factory
<br/>The word "content" is useful because it describes a lot of what we put online. But it can also change how we think. Content is something we produce to fill a space.<br/><br/>

Photography can be something else entirely. It can be slow. It can be unnecessary. It can take months to make sense. It can be something you photograph simply because you were curious.<br/><br/>

Some of the best photographs you make may have no obvious reason to exist online. There may be no trend attached to them, no useful caption, no obvious story, no impressive location, and no dramatic subject. You simply saw something, and you photographed it. That is a perfectly good reason.

<br>

## The Problem with Chasing What Works
<br/>Platforms naturally encourage repetition. If a certain photograph performs well, it is tempting to make another one - and another. Soon you have a formula.<br/><br/>

You know the type of subject that gets attention. You know the colour palette, the editing style, the framing, and the kind of caption that encourages comments. The formula works. Until it doesn't.<br/><br/>

More importantly, you can become very good at making something that looks like your work without actually exploring anything new. This is one of the great creative traps of social media: success can become restrictive. The thing that got you noticed becomes the thing you feel you have to keep doing.<br/><br/>


## Give Yourself Permission to Be Bad at Something
<br/>One of the best ways to escape this trap is to deliberately make photographs that don't fit your usual style:

<br>- Try a different focal length.
<br>- Photograph something you normally ignore.
<br>- Shoot in terrible weather.
<br>- Use a different aspect ratio.
<br>- Photograph without editing.
<br>- Try a completely different approach to black and white.
<br>- Take fifty photographs of something ordinary.
<br/><br/>
Most of them may be terrible. That's fine. Creativity needs room for bad ideas.
<br/><br/>
If every photograph has to be good enough to publish, you will naturally become conservative. You will repeat what you already know works because there is less risk involved. But experimentation requires failure. You need photographs that never leave your camera roll, photographs that don't get likes, and photographs that teach you something. Those photographs are not wasted - they are part of the work.

<br/>

## Create Before You Consume
<br/>There is another habit worth examining: how much photography do you look at before you make your own?<br/><br/>

Scroll through Instagram for thirty minutes, and you will inevitably start seeing the world through other photographers' eyes. You notice the compositions people are using, the colours they are editing with, and the subjects everyone is photographing. Then you go outside with your camera, and without realising it, you start looking for the same things.<br/><br/>

Try reversing the order. Photograph first, scroll later.<br/><br/>

Give yourself some time where your visual ideas come from the world around you rather than from your feed. You may find that your photographs become a little less predictable. That's a good thing.<br/><br/>


## Make Work That Takes Longer Than a Post<br/><br/>

Not everything needs to happen today. Start a project that doesn't have an immediate payoff:

<br>- Photograph the same street for a month.
<br>- Document a person over a year.
<br>- Return to the same place in different weather.
<br>- Make a small series around an ordinary object.
<br>- Photograph your neighbourhood without trying to make it look extraordinary.

Projects like these are difficult to optimise for an algorithm because they are not designed around immediate attention. They are designed around observation. And observation is where photography gets interesting.

<br/>

## Your Feed Is Not Your Portfolio
<br/>This is perhaps the most useful distinction to remember. Your social feed is a stream; it is designed to keep moving. Your photography can be deeper than that.<br/><br/>

Some images will get attention for a day and disappear. Others may quietly stay with you for years. Don't confuse the lifespan of a post with the lifespan of a photograph. A photograph can have very little online engagement and still be one of the most important things you've made.<br/><br/>

So keep making things that interest you. Make things that challenge you. Make things that nobody asked for. And occasionally, make something that you have absolutely no idea how to post.<br/><br/>

You might discover that those are the photographs you most wanted to make all along.
